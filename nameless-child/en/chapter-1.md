# The Village Without a Name

Some places are so small they don't appear on any map. The village where I was born was one of those places.

![The Nameless Child cover](/media/stories/nameless-child/cover.png)

There were perhaps forty families strung along a single dirt road that dissolved into rice paddies at both ends. We had no address, only a name that older people used among themselves — a name that meant something to no one outside our small world.

## The First Memory

My earliest memory is of my grandmother's hands — calloused, smelling faintly of smoke and soil — braiding my hair before the walk to school. The school was three kilometers away, past the fish pond, past the house with the large mango tree, past the field that flooded every rainy season and turned our shoes into mud-weights.

### The road we walked

We walked barefoot most days. Not because we had no shoes, but because shoes were for special occasions: Tết, a cousin's wedding, the rare trip into town.

We walked barefoot most days. Not because we had no shoes, but because shoes were for special occasions: Tết, a cousin's wedding, the rare trip into town. To wear shoes on an ordinary Tuesday would have felt wasteful, even slightly embarrassing.

## Not Knowing We Were Poor

I did not know we were poor. I knew we ate rice every meal and that meat appeared only on Sundays. I knew my mother repaired our clothes instead of replacing them. But I did not have the word *poor* yet. I only had the life we lived, which felt complete to me in the way that all lives feel complete to the people living them.

Completeness doesn't come from having much. It comes from not yet knowing what you're missing.
